Continental · 1908–1961

Maurice Merleau-Ponty

French phenomenologist of perception and the lived body

Merleau-Ponty argued that we meet the world first through the body, not as minds observing objects: perception is a bodily, pre-reflective engagement that comes before the split between subject and object.

Life and thought

Maurice Merleau-Ponty (1908–1961) studied at the École normale supérieure alongside Sartre and Beauvoir, and with them founded and edited Les Temps modernes until he broke with Sartre over communism in the early 1950s. After the war he taught psychology at Lyon and then child psychology at the Sorbonne, and in 1952 he was elected to the chair of philosophy at the Collège de France. He died suddenly of a heart attack at 53.

Merleau-Ponty argued that philosophy had wrongly started from a mind looking at objects. We meet the world first as bodies: reaching for a cup, we do not calculate positions; the hand already “knows” the way. Perception is this pre-reflective, bodily grip on things, which comes before the split into subject and object. Drawing on psychology and on patients with brain injuries, he showed how habits, gestures and the felt sense of one’s own body shape experience. His ideas now run through cognitive science and philosophy of mind.

On the record

Uncertain

Fanon attended Merleau-Ponty’s lectures while studying in Lyon.

The dates fit: Fanon studied medicine in Lyon after the war, and Merleau-Ponty taught psychology at the University of Lyon from 1945 to 1949. That Fanon sat in on his lectures is repeated in biographies and reference works, but the encyclopedia entries we checked do not confirm it; what is certain is that Black Skin, White Masks draws on Merleau-Ponty’s phenomenology of the body.
