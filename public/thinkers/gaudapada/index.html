Dialectica / Thinkers / Gauḍapāda

India · c. 6th c. CE

Gauḍapāda

Earliest known Advaita teacher, by tradition Śaṅkara’s teacher’s teacher

In verses on the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad he argued for non-origination (ajātivāda): nothing is ever truly born; the world of change is like a dream, and only non-dual consciousness is real.

Life and thought

Gauḍapāda, probably of the 6th century, is the earliest Advaita Vedānta teacher whose work survives. Tradition makes him the teacher of Govinda, who taught Śaṅkara. His Māṇḍūkya Kārikā (also called the Āgama Śāstra) is a verse commentary in four chapters on the very short Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad; scholars still debate whether all four chapters are his.

Starting from the Upaniṣad’s analysis of waking, dreaming, deep sleep and a “fourth” state of pure awareness, Gauḍapāda argues that waking objects are no more real than dream objects: both appear, both are seen, both vanish. He then argues for non-origination (ajātivāda): nothing can be born—not from itself, not from something else, not from nothing—so the world of change never truly began. Only non-dual consciousness is real. His arguments and even his vocabulary closely resemble those of Madhyamaka and Yogācāra Buddhism, which suggests a shared debate. Śaṅkara commented on his verses and built on them.

On the record

Confirmed

Gauḍapāda (c. 6th century) was by tradition the teacher of Śaṅkara’s teacher.

The Stanford Encyclopedia confirms that Śaṅkara’s teacher was named Govinda and that, according to tradition, Govinda’s teacher was Gauḍapāda, of the sixth century CE, author of the Gauḍapādakārikās.

Sources: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Śaṅkara”
