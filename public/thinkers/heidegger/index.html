Continental · 1889–1976

Martin Heidegger

German philosopher of Being, compromised by his Nazi party membership

Heidegger argued that Western philosophy forgot the question of the meaning of Being; he analysed human existence (Dasein) as being-in-the-world, care and being-towards-death, and later criticised technology for revealing everything as a resource.

Life and thought

Martin Heidegger (1889–1976) grew up in Messkirch, a small Catholic town in Baden, and first trained for the priesthood. He became Husserl’s assistant in Freiburg, made his name with Being and Time (1927) and succeeded Husserl in his chair in 1928. In 1933 he joined the Nazi party and served for a year as rector of Freiburg University; his private notebooks, published from 2014, contain antisemitic passages, and how far his politics shapes his philosophy is still debated.

Being and Time asks what it means for anything to be. Heidegger starts from Dasein, the being that we ourselves are, and argues that we are not detached minds facing objects but always already in a world of tasks and tools: a hammer shows up first as something to hammer with, not as a thing with properties. Our being is care, stretched between past and future, and facing our own death can call us back from the anonymous “they” to an authentic life. Later he argued that modern technology reveals everything, people included, as a stockpile of resources.

On the record

Confirmed

Husserl’s link to Heidegger is coded as influence, not teaching: Heidegger was his assistant, not his doctoral student.

Heidegger took his doctorate in 1913 and wrote his habilitation (1915) under the Neo-Kantian Heinrich Rickert. He became Husserl’s assistant in Freiburg in 1919 and they ran seminars together; he later dedicated Being and Time to Husserl “in friendship and admiration”. “Influence” is the accurate kind.
