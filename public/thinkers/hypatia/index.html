Hypatia

Mathematician and Neoplatonist teacher in Alexandria

Taught Platonic philosophy, astronomy and mathematics to pagan and Christian students alike and was a respected public figure. She was murdered by a Christian mob in 415 amid a political feud; none of her writings survives with certainty.

Life and thought

Hypatia was the daughter of Theon, a mathematician and astronomer in Alexandria; her birth year is unknown, perhaps between 350 and 370. She worked with her father on editions and commentaries on Ptolemy and Euclid, and led a Neoplatonic circle that taught philosophy, astronomy and mathematics to pagans and Christians alike. Her student Synesius, later a bishop, wrote her admiring letters that survive; none of her own writings does with certainty.

She taught a Platonism in the line of Plotinus, in which mathematics trains the mind to rise from the senses toward the intelligible, and she was a public figure consulted by the city’s officials. That role cost her her life. In March 415, amid a bitter feud between the prefect Orestes, her friend, and the bishop Cyril, a Christian mob led by a man named Peter seized and killed her. Her death has since been made a symbol of many things: the end of pagan learning, religious violence, and women in science.

On the record

Corrected

Hypatia was born c. 360.

Her birth year is unknown: suggestions range from about 350 to 370 (MacTutor follows Mueller’s “370?”; Maria Dzielska argued for about 355). Her death in March 415 at the hands of a Christian mob, amid the feud between the bishop Cyril and the prefect Orestes, is well attested. None of her writings survives; she worked with her father Theon on commentaries on Ptolemy and Euclid.
