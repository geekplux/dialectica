Dialectica / Thinkers / Rudolf Carnap

Analytic & pragmatist · 1891–1970

Rudolf Carnap

Vienna Circle’s great system-builder, later in Chicago and Los Angeles

Knowledge of the world can be logically reconstructed from experience, and questions like “do numbers really exist?” are not factual questions but choices of a linguistic framework, to be judged by their usefulness.

Life and thought

Rudolf Carnap (1891–1970) studied physics and philosophy at Jena, where he attended Frege’s logic lectures, and served in the First World War. He joined the Vienna Circle in 1926 and became its most systematic thinker, taught in Prague from 1931, and in 1935 emigrated to the United States, teaching at Chicago and later at UCLA.

In The Logical Structure of the World (1928) he tried to show how all our concepts of the world could be built up, by logic alone, from elementary experiences. He later argued that a question such as “do numbers really exist?” is not a question of fact. Asked inside a linguistic framework, such as arithmetic, it is trivial; asked from outside, it is a practical choice of which framework to use, to be judged by its usefulness. In a 1932 essay he held up Heidegger’s sentence “the Nothing itself nothings” as a model of a meaningless pseudo-statement. Quine, who admired him, attacked his distinction between analytic and synthetic truths, and their long exchange shaped mid-century analytic philosophy.

On the record

Confirmed

Carnap both shaped Quine and was the main target of his critique, so the atlas draws two ties between them.

Quine’s 1932–33 fellowship took him to Prague, where Carnap was teaching; the Stanford Encyclopedia names the Logical Empiricists, “especially Carnap”, as Quine’s strongest influence, and reads “Two Dogmas of Empiricism” (1951) as largely a debate with Carnap. Both ties stand.

Sources: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Willard Van Orman Quine”
