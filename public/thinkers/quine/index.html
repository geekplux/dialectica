Analytic & pragmatist · 1908–2000

W. V. O. Quine

Harvard logician who attacked the analytic–synthetic distinction

There is no sharp line between truths of meaning and truths of fact; our beliefs form a single web that faces experience as a whole, and philosophy is continuous with natural science.

Life and thought

Willard Van Orman Quine (1908–2000) studied mathematics at Oberlin and wrote a Harvard doctorate on Principia Mathematica in two years. A travelling fellowship in 1932–33 took him to Vienna, Warsaw and Prague, where long discussions with Carnap began the most important philosophical relationship of his life. After serving in US naval intelligence in the Second World War, he taught at Harvard for the rest of his career.

In “Two Dogmas of Empiricism” (1951) Quine denied that there is a principled line between analytic truths, true by meaning alone, and synthetic truths, true because of the facts. Our beliefs form a single web that faces experience as a whole: when a prediction fails, we could revise anything, even logic, though we prefer to adjust the edges rather than the centre. Philosophy is therefore continuous with science, not a court above it. He also argued that translation is indeterminate: several incompatible ways of translating a stranger’s word “gavagai” could fit all the evidence of how the speaker behaves.

On the record

Confirmed

Carnap both shaped Quine and was the main target of his critique, so the atlas draws two ties between them.

Quine’s 1932–33 fellowship took him to Prague, where Carnap was teaching; the Stanford Encyclopedia names the Logical Empiricists, “especially Carnap”, as Quine’s strongest influence, and reads “Two Dogmas of Empiricism” (1951) as largely a debate with Carnap. Both ties stand.

Sources: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Willard Van Orman Quine”
