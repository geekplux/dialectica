Early modern Europe · 1724–1804

Immanuel Kant

Königsberg professor whose critical philosophy redrew modern thought

The mind actively structures experience, so we can know appearances with certainty but never things in themselves. Morality is a law reason gives itself: act only on a maxim you could will to be a universal law.

Life and thought

Immanuel Kant spent his whole life in and around Königsberg in East Prussia, the son of a Pietist harness-maker. For decades he lectured at its university on everything from physics and logic to geography and anthropology. After a long “silent decade” he published the Critique of Pure Reason in 1781, at fifty-seven, and over the next ten years the works that reshaped ethics, aesthetics and political thought.

Kant’s “Copernican revolution” reverses the usual picture: instead of the mind conforming to objects, objects as we experience them must conform to the mind’s forms — space, time and categories such as cause and effect. That explains how we can know some things a priori, such as that every event has a cause, but it limits knowledge to appearances: God, freedom and the immortal soul lie beyond proof, though not beyond rational faith. In ethics, the moral law comes neither from God nor from the pursuit of happiness but from reason itself: act only on a maxim you could will as a universal law, and treat humanity never merely as a means. Almost every later school defines itself by how it answers him.

Corrected

The note said Peirce studied the Critique of Pure Reason for hours every day for more than three years.

The Stanford Encyclopedia confirms that Peirce began Kant in 1855, his first year at Harvard, spent about three years in intense study of the Critique, and kept rereading it all his life, though he came to judge its logic flawed. The exact daily-hours wording could not be checked in a source, so the note now states what is documented.
