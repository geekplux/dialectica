Analytic & pragmatist · 1921–2002

John Rawls

Harvard philosopher who revived political philosophy with justice as fairness

Principles of justice are those free and equal people would choose behind a veil of ignorance: equal basic liberties for all, and social and economic inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged.

Life and thought

John Rawls (1921–2002) grew up in Baltimore, where two of his younger brothers died of illnesses caught from him. He served as an infantryman in the Pacific in the Second World War, an experience that ended his plans for the priesthood, and after a doctorate at Princeton he taught at Harvard from 1962. A Theory of Justice (1971), twenty years in the making, revived political philosophy in the English-speaking world.

Rawls asks which principles of justice we would choose if we did not know our place in society: our talents, class, race or idea of the good life. In this “original position”, behind a “veil of ignorance”, he argued, we would choose, first, equal basic liberties for everyone, and second, that social and economic inequalities are acceptable only under fair equality of opportunity and only if they benefit the least advantaged (the “difference principle”). Against utilitarianism, no one’s basic rights may be sacrificed for a greater total good. Nozick replied from the right and Nussbaum from the left; Political Liberalism (1993) asked how people with deeply different beliefs can share one just society.
