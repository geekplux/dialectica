Medieval Europe · c. 477–524 or 526

Boethius

Roman statesman who translated Aristotle’s logic into Latin

God’s foreknowledge does not destroy free will, because God is eternal and sees all times at once, as a present. He also posed the medieval problem of universals.

Life and thought

Boethius was a Roman aristocrat born around 477–480, a generation after the last western emperor. Orphaned young, he was raised in the household of the senator Symmachus, became consul in 510 and later chief of the palace offices under the Ostrogothic king Theodoric. Accused of treason, he was imprisoned and executed around 524 or 526.

He had planned to translate all of Plato and Aristotle into Latin and show that they agreed; he finished Aristotle’s logic, with commentaries, and for six centuries this was nearly all the Aristotle the Latin West had. His commentary on Porphyry posed the question that ran through medieval philosophy: are genera and species real things or only concepts? In prison he wrote the Consolation of Philosophy, in which Philosophy, as a woman, shows him that fortune’s gifts were never his, and that God’s foreknowledge does not destroy freedom, because God sees all times in one eternal present.

On the record

Contested

Boethius lived c. 480–524.

Reference works differ: the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy gives c. 480–524, the Stanford Encyclopedia c. 475–77 to probably 526. All agree that he wrote the Consolation of Philosophy in prison shortly before his execution under Theodoric. His two commentaries on Porphyry’s Isagoge are undated but belong to his translation project, much of which was done by about 518.

Sources: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius” · Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Boethius”
