Dialectica / Thinkers / Hui Shi

China & East Asia · c. 370–310 BCE

Hui Shi

Wei statesman, logician and Zhuangzi’s debating partner

His ten paradoxes, preserved only in the Zhuangzi, argue that size, place and time are relative (“I set off for Yue today and arrived yesterday”), ending in “love all things; heaven and earth are one body”. His own writings are lost.

Life and thought

Hui Shi served King Hui of Wei as chancellor around the 310s BCE and was a celebrated disputer; the Zhuangzi says his books would have filled five carts. None survive. We know him chiefly through his friend and rival Zhuangzi, who records their arguments, and through a list of ten theses in the “All Under Heaven” chapter, written by an early Han author.

The theses play with the relativity of measures and places: “The largest has nothing outside it; the smallest has nothing inside it”; “the sun at noon is the sun setting”; “the south has no limit and yet has a limit”. If size, place and time depend on the standpoint from which they are judged, the distinctions people fight over are not absolute, and the list ends: “love all things; heaven and earth are one body”. Zhuangzi teased him for clever words, yet mourned him: after Hui Shi died, he said, he had no one left to talk with.

On the record

Uncertain

Hui Shi is shown as an influence on Gongsun Long.

Both are counted among the disputers (bianzhe) and the later tradition groups them as the School of Names, but no source records that they met or that Gongsun Long learned from Hui Shi. The tie marks a shared line of argument, not a documented contact.
