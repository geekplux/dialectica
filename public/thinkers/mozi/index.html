Dialectica / Thinkers / Mozi

China & East Asia · c. 470–391 BCE

Mozi

Founder of Mohism and critic of Confucian partiality

Argued for impartial care and against offensive war and costly rituals, judging every doctrine by its benefit to the people; he is often called China’s first consequentialist.

Life and thought

Mo Di flourished around 430 BCE, probably in Song or Lu; Sima Qian calls him an official of Song, and later tradition links him with craftsmen. He led a disciplined band of followers who studied argument and also the defence of cities: the Mozi tells how he walked ten days and nights to dissuade Chu from attacking Song, out-manoeuvring the engineer Gongshu Ban in a model siege. His teachings survive in chapters written down by his followers.

Mozi asked a simple question of every custom: does it benefit the people? Wars, theft and feuds arise because people care for their own family or state and not others’. If everyone regarded others’ states as their own, who would attack them? Hence impartial care (jian’ai), the condemnation of offensive war, and attacks on costly funerals and court music. He proposed three tests for any doctrine: its basis in the sage kings, the evidence of ordinary people’s eyes and ears, and its benefit in use. For two centuries Mohism rivalled Confucianism; then it vanished, to be rediscovered in the Qing.

On the record

Contested

Mozi, Shang Yang, Hui Shi, Gongsun Long, Zhuangzi and Xunzi are given conventional birth and death years.

None of these dates is secure. Current reference works give: Mozi flourished c. 430 BCE; Shang Yang c. 390–338 BCE; Hui Shi flourished 313 BCE; Gongsun Long c. 320–250 BCE; Zhuangzi flourished in the latter half of the 4th century BCE; Xunzi was born perhaps c. 310 BCE and was still alive in 238 BCE. The atlas’s ranges fall within these and are marked “c.”.
