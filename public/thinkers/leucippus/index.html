Dialectica / Thinkers / Leucippus

Greece & Rome · 5th c. BCE

Leucippus

Founder of atomism, about whom almost nothing is known

Proposed that the world consists of indivisible atoms moving in the void, and that “nothing happens at random, but everything for a reason and by necessity”. Epicurus is reported to have doubted that he existed at all.

Life and thought

Leucippus is the most shadowy figure in early Greek philosophy. Ancient writers say he came from Miletus, Elea or Abdera, studied with Zeno of Elea and taught Democritus; Epicurus apparently doubted that he existed at all. But Aristotle and Theophrastus treat him as the real founder of atomism, active in the 5th century BCE, and one sentence of his, from a work On Mind, survives.

His idea answered Parmenides directly. Accept that what is cannot come from nothing or vanish into nothing, but deny that there is no void. Then reality can be countless tiny, solid, indivisible bodies, atoms, moving through empty space, and every apparent birth, death and change is just atoms joining and separating. Even whole worlds form this way, in whirls of atoms. His surviving sentence states the programme: nothing happens at random, but everything for a reason and by necessity. It is the first thoroughly mechanical picture of nature.

On the record

Uncertain

Leucippus founded atomism in the 5th century BCE; Epicurus is reported to have doubted he existed.

Nothing firm is known of his dates or birthplace; ancient reports make him a pupil of Zeno of Elea and the teacher of Democritus. Epicurus apparently denied there had been a philosopher Leucippus, and some 19th-century scholars agreed, but Aristotle and Theophrastus treat him as real. The one surviving sentence, from a work On Mind, says nothing happens in vain (or at random) but everything from reason and by necessity.

Sources: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Leucippus”
