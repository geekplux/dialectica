Dialectica / Thinkers / B. R. Ambedkar

India · 1891–1956

B. R. Ambedkar

Dalit leader, jurist and chief architect of India’s constitution

He argued that caste is a system of graded inequality sanctioned by Hindu scripture and must be annihilated, not reformed. In 1956 he converted to Buddhism with hundreds of thousands of followers, recasting it as a rational, social ethic (Navayana).

Life and thought

Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar (1891–1956) was born into the Mahar caste, treated as untouchable, and endured segregation at school. With scholarships he studied at Columbia University, where John Dewey was among his teachers, and at the London School of Economics, earning doctorates in economics and qualifying as a barrister. He led Dalit movements, clashed with Gandhi over separate electorates in 1932, chaired the drafting committee of India’s constitution, and served as independent India’s first law minister.

Ambedkar argued that caste is not a division of labour but a division of labourers—a hierarchy of graded inequality held in place by religious sanction—so it cannot be reformed from within Hinduism: the scriptures that sanction it must lose their authority. That is the case of Annihilation of Caste (1936), written as a speech its organisers cancelled. In October 1956, weeks before his death, he converted to Buddhism at Nagpur with hundreds of thousands of followers, presenting it in The Buddha and His Dhamma as a rational, egalitarian social ethic.

On the record

Confirmed

Ambedkar studied under John Dewey at Columbia University (1913–16) and drew on his ideas of democracy.

Ambedkar studied at Columbia from 1913 to 1916 (MA 1915) and in Annihilation of Caste (1936) calls ‘Prof. John Dewey, who was my teacher and to whom I owe so much’. His formal dissertation adviser was the economist Edwin Seligman, so ‘taught’ means he took Dewey’s classes, not that Dewey supervised him.
