Greece & Rome · c. 428–348 BCE

Plato

Socrates’ student, founder of the Academy, master of the dialogue

Argued that true knowledge is of eternal Forms, above all the Form of the Good, and that a just soul and a just city both need reason to rule. He wrote dialogues, so his own views must be read through his characters.

Life and thought

Plato was born into an aristocratic Athenian family around 428 BCE and grew up during the Peloponnesian War. He expected a political career, but the oligarchy of 404, which included his relatives, and then the execution of Socrates in 399 turned him away from Athenian politics. After travels that took him to Sicily, he founded the Academy around 387, where Aristotle studied for twenty years. All his known works survive, nearly all as dialogues in which he himself never speaks.

His central idea is that the things we see are unstable copies, and that knowledge is of something stable: Forms, such as Justice itself or Equality itself, grasped by reason rather than the senses. The Republic’s cave image shows people mistaking shadows for reality, and the philosopher as the one who turns around. The soul, akin to the Forms, is immortal, and a just person is one whose reason rules spirit and appetite; a just city mirrors that order. Nearly every later philosophy has defined itself for or against Plato.

On the record

Confirmed

The years given for Plato’s dialogues (Symposium c. 385, Phaedo c. 380, Republic c. 375 BCE) are approximate.

No dialogue is securely dated. The usual early, middle and late grouping rests on ancient testimony that the Laws came last and on stylometric study of the Greek; it remains disputed whether it reflects the order of composition. The atlas’s years follow the common view that the Symposium, Phaedo and Republic belong to the middle period.
