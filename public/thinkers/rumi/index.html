The Islamic world · 1207–1273

Jalāl al-Dīn Rūmī

Persian poet and Sufi teacher in Konya

The soul is a reed cut from its reed-bed and longs to return; love, not reason alone, is the way back to God.

Life and thought

Jalāl al-Dīn Rūmī was born in 1207 in Central Asia, in Balkh or nearby Vakhsh, and as a child left with his family, perhaps ahead of the Mongol invasions, for Anatolia, where his father became a preacher in Konya. Rumi succeeded him as a respected teacher of religious law. In 1244 he met Shams of Tabriz, a wandering dervish, and the friendship overturned his life: he turned to poetry, music and ecstatic dance. After Shams disappeared for good around 1248, Rumi poured out thousands of lyric poems in his name. He died in Konya in 1273, and his followers founded the Mevlevi order, known for its whirling ceremony.

His Dīwān-e Shams gathers those lyrics. His Masnavi, six books of rhymed couplets, teaches Sufi ideas through stories, jokes and parables. It opens with a reed flute complaining that it was cut from its reed-bed: the soul, separated from its source, longs to return. The way back is love, which consumes the self; reason alone, like people feeling an elephant in a dark room, grasps only parts.

On the record

Confirmed

The Dīwān-e Shams dates from about 1250.

It is not a single book written at one time: Rumi composed these ghazals from his meeting with Shams of Tabriz in 1244 and above all after Shams vanished in 1247–48, continuing into the late 1250s. His students collected them after his death; the standard edition by Forūzānfar has about 3,229 ghazals, some of doubtful authenticity. “c. 1250” marks the heart of that period.

Sources: Wikipedia, “Divan-i Shams-i Tabrizi”
