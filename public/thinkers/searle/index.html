Analytic & pragmatist · 1932–2025

John Searle

Berkeley philosopher of language and mind, author of the Chinese Room

A person who follows rules to shuffle Chinese symbols could answer questions in Chinese without understanding a word, so running a program is not the same as having a mind; understanding depends on the causal powers of brains.

Life and thought

John Searle (1932–2025) was born in Denver and went to Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar, writing his doctorate there under J. L. Austin and Peter Strawson. From 1959 he taught at the University of California, Berkeley, where in 1964 he was the first tenured professor to back the Free Speech Movement. In 2019 the university revoked his emeritus status after finding that he had violated its sexual-harassment policies. He died in September 2025, aged 93.

Searle turned Austin’s lectures into a systematic theory of speech acts (1969). His best-known argument, the Chinese Room (1980), asks you to imagine sitting in a room with an English rulebook for matching Chinese symbols to other Chinese symbols. People outside receive fluent answers to their questions in Chinese, yet you understand nothing. A computer running a program does only what you do, manipulating symbols by their shapes, so a program alone cannot produce understanding. Minds, he held, are biological features caused by brain processes (“biological naturalism”). Critics, Dennett among them, replied that the whole system understands even if the man inside does not.
