Greece & Rome · c. 515 – after 450 BCE

Parmenides

Founder of the Eleatic school and of deductive argument

In his poem a goddess reveals that only “what is” can be thought, so being is one, eternal, unchanging and whole. The “way of opinion”, full of change, is presented as deceptive.

Life and thought

Parmenides lived in Elea, a Greek colony on the west coast of southern Italy. Plato has him visit Athens at about sixty-five and talk with a young Socrates, which would put his birth around 515 BCE, and ancient reports say he gave his city laws. He wrote a poem in epic verse in which an unnamed goddess shows a young man two paths: the way of truth and the way of mortal opinion.

The way of truth is an argument. You cannot think or say what is not, since there would be nothing to think. So there is no coming-to-be from what is not and no perishing into it, no gaps of emptiness and no change: what is must be one, whole, unmoving and complete. If our senses report a changing, many-coloured world, so much the worse for the senses. It was the first sustained deductive argument in Western thought, and every later Greek physics, from the pluralists and atomists to Plato, had to answer it.

On the record

Contested

Parmenides’ attack on “two-headed mortals” is often, though debatably, read as aimed at Heraclitus.

Fragment 6 condemns mortals for whom “to be and not to be are the same and not the same” but names no one. Some scholars hear Heraclitus in it; others see a general target, and recent work stresses how much the two thinkers share. Even their relative dates are uncertain.
