Martha Nussbaum

Chicago philosopher of the capabilities approach and the emotions

Justice should secure for every person a threshold of central capabilities, such as health, education, play and political voice; drawing on Aristotle and the Greek tragedians, she argues that emotions and vulnerability belong to ethics.

Life and thought

Martha Nussbaum (born 1947 in New York) studied theatre and classics before writing a Harvard doctorate on Aristotle. She taught at Harvard and Brown and since 1995 has been at the University of Chicago, where she holds a chair in law and ethics. Her collaboration with the economist Amartya Sen at the UN’s development research institute in Helsinki shaped the “capabilities approach”, and in 2026 the Library of Congress awarded her the Kluge Prize.

The Fragility of Goodness (1986) draws on Greek tragedy and philosophy to argue that a good human life is exposed to luck, and that emotions are not blind forces but judgements about what matters. In politics she asks not how much wealth people have but what they are actually able to do and to be. A just society must secure for every person a threshold of ten central capabilities, among them life, health, bodily integrity, practical reason, play, affiliation and some control over one’s environment. Frontiers of Justice (2006) argues that social-contract theories like Rawls’s leave out people with disabilities, citizens of other nations and animals.
