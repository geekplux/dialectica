Dialectica / Thinkers / Mohandas K. Gandhi

India · 1869–1948

Mohandas K. Gandhi

Leader of Indian independence and theorist of non-violent resistance

He developed satyagraha, ‘holding firmly to truth’: resisting injustice through non-violent non-cooperation and willingly accepted suffering, on the conviction that means shape ends and that truth is God.

Life and thought

Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (1869–1948) grew up in Gujarat in a devout Vaiṣṇava family in a region with a strong Jain presence, trained as a barrister in London, and spent twenty-one years in South Africa, where racial discrimination turned a shy lawyer into an organiser of mass resistance. Back in India from 1915, he led the national movement through campaigns such as the Salt March of 1930. He was assassinated by a Hindu nationalist in January 1948, months after independence.

Gandhi’s philosophy was worked out in action. Satyagraha, “holding fast to truth”, is resistance that refuses both submission and violence: the resister breaks an unjust law openly, accepts the penalty, and seeks to convert the opponent rather than defeat him. Behind it lie the convictions that means and ends cannot be separated, that truth is God, and that self-rule (swaraj) begins with ruling oneself. In Hind Swaraj (1909) he turned this into a critique of modern industrial civilisation. Ambedkar fought him over caste; Martin Luther King Jr. adopted his method.

On the record

Confirmed

Gandhi’s non-violence drew on Gujarat’s Jain culture, especially his Jain friend and mentor Raychandbhai.

Gandhi’s Autobiography devotes a chapter to Raychandbhai (Shrimad Rajchandra), the Jain poet and thinker he met on landing in Bombay in 1891. He names him first among ‘three moderns’ who left a deep impress on his life, with Tolstoy and Ruskin, and calls him his refuge in moments of spiritual crisis, though not his guru.

Sources: M. K. Gandhi, An Autobiography, Part II, ch. 1, “Raychandbhai”
