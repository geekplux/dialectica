Analytic & pragmatist · 1902–1994

Karl Popper

Viennese-born philosopher of science and defender of the open society

A theory is scientific only if it could in principle be refuted by observation; knowledge grows by conjectures and refutations, and societies, like science, flourish when they stay open to criticism.

Life and thought

Karl Popper (1902–1994) grew up in Vienna in an assimilated family of Jewish origin, trained as a cabinet-maker and a schoolteacher, and moved on the edge of the Vienna Circle without joining it. His Logik der Forschung (1934) brought him a post in New Zealand, where he wrote The Open Society and Its Enemies during the Second World War; from 1946 he taught at the London School of Economics.

Popper accepted Hume’s point that no number of white swans proves that all swans are white, while one black swan refutes it. Science therefore does not prove theories by induction: it proposes bold conjectures and tries hard to refute them. A theory is scientific if it forbids something observable and so could turn out false. Popper thought that Freudian psychoanalysis and Marx’s theory of history, as their followers defended them, could explain any outcome and so tested nothing. His politics follow the same logic: an open society corrects its mistakes by criticism and piecemeal reform, whereas Plato, Hegel and Marx promised certainty and pointed toward closed societies.
