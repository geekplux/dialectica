Continental · 1820–1895

Friedrich Engels

Manufacturer’s son who co-founded Marxism with Marx

Engels documented industrial poverty first-hand, financed and edited Marx’s work, and popularised Marxism as a scientific worldview, extending the dialectic to nature and to the origins of the family.

Life and thought

Friedrich Engels (1820–1895) was the eldest son of a cotton-mill owner in Barmen, Prussia. Sent in 1842 to the family firm’s mill near Manchester, he saw the industrial slums at first hand and wrote The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845). He began his lifelong partnership with Marx in Paris in 1844, co-wrote The Communist Manifesto, and from 1850 worked in the Manchester business for two decades, partly to support Marx.

Engels was more than Marx’s patron. After Marx died he edited the second and third volumes of Capital from the manuscripts, and in Anti-Dühring and other works he presented Marxism as a scientific worldview, extending the dialectic from society to nature. The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State argued that women’s subordination arose with private property and the patriarchal family, a thesis feminists later debated. Much of what the twentieth century called “Marxism” reached it through his popularisations.

On the record

Confirmed

Engels is described as a manufacturer’s son.

His father, Friedrich Engels Sr., was a prosperous cotton-mill owner in Barmen who co-founded the firm Ermen & Engels in 1837, later with a thread factory in Manchester. Engels worked for the firm in Manchester from 1842 and again from 1850, became a partner in 1864 and retired in 1869.
