Continental · 1859–1938

Edmund Husserl

Moravian-born mathematician turned philosopher, founder of phenomenology

Husserl held that philosophy must become a rigorous science of consciousness: by bracketing our natural belief in the world, we can describe the intentional structures through which anything appears.

Life and thought

Edmund Husserl (1859–1938) was born into a Jewish family in Prossnitz, Moravia, took a doctorate in mathematics and turned to philosophy after hearing Franz Brentano in Vienna. He taught at Halle, Göttingen and, from 1916, Freiburg, where Heidegger became his assistant. After 1933 the Nazi regime treated him as a Jew and cut him off from academic life; after his death some 40,000 pages of his manuscripts were smuggled to Leuven, where they are still kept.

Husserl wanted philosophy to become a rigorous science by returning “to the things themselves”, as they show up in experience. Consciousness, he held, is always consciousness of something (intentionality). To study it we perform the epoché: we bracket the everyday belief that the world exists independently, not to doubt it but to describe how a tree, a number or another person appears to us, and through which acts of perceiving, remembering or imagining. His method launched phenomenology and shaped Heidegger, Sartre, Merleau-Ponty and Levinas.

On the record

Confirmed

Husserl’s link to Heidegger is coded as influence, not teaching: Heidegger was his assistant, not his doctoral student.

Heidegger took his doctorate in 1913 and wrote his habilitation (1915) under the Neo-Kantian Heinrich Rickert. He became Husserl’s assistant in Freiburg in 1919 and they ran seminars together; he later dedicated Being and Time to Husserl “in friendship and admiration”. “Influence” is the accurate kind.
