Continental · born 1942 · living

Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak

Indian-born theorist and translator of Derrida, at Columbia University

Spivak asked whether the subaltern, those shut out of power and representation, can speak: well-meaning intellectuals who claim to speak for them often silence them again.

Life and thought

Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak (born 1942) grew up in Calcutta, graduated from Presidency College in 1959 and went to the United States to study comparative literature at Cornell, where she wrote her doctorate on W. B. Yeats under Paul de Man. Her translation of Derrida’s Of Grammatology (1976), with its long preface, introduced deconstruction to many English readers. She has taught at Columbia since 1991, became a University Professor there in 2007, and has long run rural schools in West Bengal; she received the Holberg Prize in 2025.

“Can the Subaltern Speak?” asks whether those at the bottom of colonial and postcolonial society, above all poor women, can make themselves heard. Her example is sati, widow-burning: British officials claimed to be saving women from their men, while Indian traditionalists claimed the widows chose to die, and in both stories the women’s own voices vanish. Her answer, that the subaltern cannot speak, means not that they are silent but that no structure exists to hear them, and that speaking for them can repeat the silencing.

On the record

Confirmed

Spivak’s “Can the Subaltern Speak?” is dated 1988.

The standard text appeared in 1988 in Cary Nelson and Lawrence Grossberg (eds.), Marxism and the Interpretation of Culture (pp. 271–313). It is reported to have grown out of a 1983 talk, and Spivak revised it again in A Critique of Postcolonial Reason (1999).
