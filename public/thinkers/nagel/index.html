Thomas Nagel

NYU philosopher who asked what it is like to be a bat

An organism is conscious if there is something it is like to be it; this subjective point of view cannot be captured by any objective, physical description, the “view from nowhere”.

Life and thought

Thomas Nagel (born 1937 in Belgrade) grew up in the United States, studied at Cornell and at Oxford, and wrote his doctorate at Harvard under John Rawls. After teaching at Berkeley and Princeton he moved in 1980 to New York University, where he became University Professor. He writes in a clear, unspecialised style, and his short book What Does It All Mean? (1987) is a classic introduction to philosophy.

In “What Is It Like to Be a Bat?” (1974) Nagel points out that bats perceive the world by echolocation. We could learn every physical fact about a bat’s brain and still not know what it is like, for the bat, to experience the world that way. Experience is tied to a point of view, and physical science works by abstracting from points of view, so it leaves something out. The tension between the subjective and the objective, the “view from nowhere”, runs through his work on ethics, free will and the meaning of life. In Mind and Cosmos (2012) he argued that materialist neo-Darwinism cannot explain consciousness, a claim many scientists rejected.
