Analytic & pragmatist · 1872–1970

Bertrand Russell

Logician, public intellectual and Nobel laureate; lectured in China 1920–21

Logical analysis reveals what our sentences really assert, as in his theory of descriptions; he tried to found mathematics on logic and discovered the paradox that undermined Frege’s system.

Life and thought

Bertrand Russell (1872–1970), grandson of a British prime minister, was orphaned young and raised by his grandmother. At Cambridge in 1898 he and G. E. Moore broke with the idealism then dominant, and with Alfred North Whitehead he wrote Principia Mathematica (1910–13), an attempt to derive mathematics from logic. He campaigned all his life: he was jailed in 1918 for opposing the First World War, lectured in China in 1920–21, won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1950, and in his eighties led the campaign against nuclear weapons.

Russell’s method was analysis: grammar can hide the logical form of what we say. In “On Denoting” (1905) he asked what “the present King of France is bald” is about, since there is no such king. His answer: the sentence really says that there is exactly one King of France and that he is bald, so it is simply false, and no strange non-existent object is needed. The paradox he found in Frege’s system, the set of all sets that are not members of themselves, reshaped the foundations of mathematics.

Corrected

The note quoted Russell as following Moore’s rebellion against idealism “with a sense of emancipation”.

That wording could not be found in Russell’s writings. His account in My Philosophical Development dates the revolt against Kant and Hegel to late 1898: “Moore led the way, but I followed closely in his footsteps.” The note now quotes this.

Sources: Richard L. Cartwright, “Russell and Moore, 1898–1905”, in The Cambridge Companion to Bertrand Russell (2003)
