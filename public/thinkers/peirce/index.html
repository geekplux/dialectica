Dialectica / Thinkers / Charles Sanders Peirce

Analytic & pragmatist · 1839–1914

Charles Sanders Peirce

Logician and scientist who founded pragmatism and semiotics

To make an idea clear, ask what practical effects its object could conceivably have; truth is the opinion that endless inquiry by a community of investigators would finally agree on.

Life and thought

Charles Sanders Peirce (1839–1914) grew up in Cambridge, Massachusetts, the son of the Harvard mathematician Benjamin Peirce, and trained as a chemist. For about three decades he worked for the US Coast Survey, measuring gravity with pendulums, while writing on logic. He taught logic at Johns Hopkins from 1879 to 1884, lost the post, and never held another academic job; he died poor in Milford, Pennsylvania, leaving a vast mass of unpublished manuscripts.

In “How to Make Our Ideas Clear” (1878) he proposed a test for meaning: our idea of a thing is our idea of its practical effects. To call something “hard” is to say that many other things will not scratch it. Truth is what an unending community of inquirers would finally agree on, and inquiry starts from real doubt, not Descartes’ pretended doubt. He also founded a general theory of signs and, independently of Frege, a logic of quantifiers. When William James made “pragmatism” famous, Peirce renamed his own version “pragmaticism” to keep it apart.

On the record

Corrected

The note said Peirce studied the Critique of Pure Reason for hours every day for more than three years.

The Stanford Encyclopedia confirms that Peirce began Kant in 1855, his first year at Harvard, spent about three years in intense study of the Critique, and kept rereading it all his life, though he came to judge its logic flawed. The exact daily-hours wording could not be checked in a source, so the note now states what is documented.

Sources: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Charles Sanders Peirce”

Confirmed

Dewey took Peirce’s logic course at Johns Hopkins; the atlas records this as influence rather than teaching.

As a graduate student at Johns Hopkins (1882–84) Dewey studied logic with Peirce but found it “too mathematical” and did not pursue it; Peirce had no sizable impact on him then, though Dewey later credited Peirce’s pragmatism as an important influence. “Influenced” is the right kind of tie.

Sources: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “John Dewey”
