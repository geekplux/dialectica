Dialectica / Thinkers / Heraclitus

Greece & Rome · c. 535–475 BCE

Heraclitus

Riddling sage of Ephesus who saw the world as ordered flux

Taught that all things flow and that opposites are one, held together by the logos, a hidden rational order; “you cannot step into the same river twice” is a later paraphrase. Fire, ever-changing yet kept in measure, is his image of the cosmos.

Life and thought

Heraclitus came from an old aristocratic family of Ephesus and was active around 500 BCE. He is said to have given up a hereditary office to his brother and to have deposited his single book, probably a collection of sharp, riddling sayings, in the temple of Artemis. He scorned the crowd and famous predecessors alike: Pythagoras’ “much learning”, he said, does not teach understanding.

His central claim is that the world is a unity of opposites held in tension by the logos, an order that is always there but that most people fail to notice. The road up and the road down are one and the same; sea water is drinkable for fish and deadly for people. Change is not the enemy of order but its form: fire, “kindled in measures and extinguished in measures”, is his image of the cosmos. The line about never stepping into the same river twice is Plato’s paraphrase. The Stoics later made his logos and fire the core of their physics.

On the record

Confirmed

“You cannot step into the same river twice” is a later paraphrase of Heraclitus.

The line comes from Plato’s Cratylus (402a). Heraclitus’ authentic river fragment (B12) says something subtler: on those who step into the same rivers, other and other waters flow. He reportedly deposited a single book, probably a collection of sayings, in the temple of Artemis at Ephesus.

Sources: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Heraclitus”

Contested

Parmenides’ attack on “two-headed mortals” is often, though debatably, read as aimed at Heraclitus.

Fragment 6 condemns mortals for whom “to be and not to be are the same and not the same” but names no one. Some scholars hear Heraclitus in it; others see a general target, and recent work stresses how much the two thinkers share. Even their relative dates are uncertain.

Sources: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Parmenides” · Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Heraclitus”
