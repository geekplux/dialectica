Early modern Europe · 1788–1860

Arthur Schopenhauer

German pessimist who saw a blind will behind all things

Behind the world as representation lies one blind, insatiable striving — the will — that makes life swing between pain and boredom. Relief comes through art, compassion and the denial of the will, ideas he found echoed in Indian thought.

Life and thought

Arthur Schopenhauer was the son of a wealthy Danzig merchant and of the novelist Johanna Schopenhauer. After his father’s death he abandoned a business career, studied at Göttingen and Berlin, where Fichte was among his teachers, and published The World as Will and Representation at the end of 1818, aged thirty. His attempt to teach in Berlin failed when he scheduled his lectures at the same hour as Hegel’s. Living on his inheritance in Frankfurt, he was largely ignored until a collection of essays, Parerga and Paralipomena (1851), made him famous in his last decade.

Schopenhauer accepted Kant’s division between appearance and thing in itself, but claimed we know the thing in itself from within: in our own bodies we experience it as will — blind, endless striving. The whole world is this one will, appearing as countless things that struggle against each other. Because desire is never satisfied for long, life swings between pain and boredom. Relief comes through art, above all music, through compassion, which recognises the same suffering in others, and through ascetic denial of the will — ideas he found echoed in Buddhism and the Upanishads. Nietzsche, Wagner and Freud read him closely.

On the record

Confirmed

Schopenhauer scheduled his Berlin lectures at the same hour as Hegel’s.

In March 1820 Schopenhauer, newly licensed to teach in Berlin, scheduled his class at the same time as Hegel’s popular lectures; few students came. He had studied in Berlin in 1811–13, where his lecturers included Fichte.

Sources: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Arthur Schopenhauer”
