Continental · 1908–1986

Simone de Beauvoir

French existentialist whose The Second Sex reshaped feminism

Beauvoir argued that women have been made into man’s “Other”, defined by men rather than by themselves; since there is no feminine essence, freedom means refusing that assigned situation.

Life and thought

Simone de Beauvoir (1908–1986) grew up in a Catholic bourgeois family in Paris whose fortunes declined, and decided early to earn her own living. In 1929 she passed the agrégation in philosophy, placed second only to Sartre, with whom she formed a lifelong, unmarried partnership. She taught in lycées until 1943, then wrote novels, philosophy and memoirs; The Mandarins won the Prix Goncourt in 1954.

In The Ethics of Ambiguity she argued that existentialist freedom is not licence: I can only will my own freedom by also willing the freedom of others. The Second Sex applied this to women. Men have made themselves the Subject and women the Other, defined by and for men; biology, myths, upbringing and marriage turn a female human being into a “woman”. Hence “one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman”. Women’s freedom means economic independence and refusing that assigned role. The book became a founding text of second-wave feminism.

On the record

Contested

Beauvoir influenced Sartre’s philosophy.

How far is debated. Beauvoir herself called her philosophy an elaboration of Sartre’s, but since the 1990s scholars have asked whether ideas in Being and Nothingness (1943) first appeared in her novel She Came to Stay (1943); her diaries show ideas developed before, alongside and often against his.
