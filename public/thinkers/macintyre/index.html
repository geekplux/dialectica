Dialectica / Thinkers / Alasdair MacIntyre

Analytic & pragmatist · 1929–2025

Alasdair MacIntyre

Scottish-born philosopher who moved from Marxism to Thomism

Modern moral debate is interminable because it uses fragments of older traditions without their context; virtues make sense only within practices, the narrative unity of a life, and a living tradition.

Life and thought

Alasdair MacIntyre (1929–2025) was born in Glasgow and educated in London, Manchester and Oxford. As a young man he was a Marxist, a member of the Communist Party until 1956 and then of Trotskyist groups. He moved to the United States around 1970, taught at several universities and from the 1980s mainly at Notre Dame, with a spell at Duke, retiring in 2010. In the early 1980s he became a Catholic and a Thomist. He died in May 2025, aged 96.

After Virtue (1981) opens with a parable: imagine that after a catastrophe people go on using the words of a lost natural science without understanding them. Modern moral language, he argues, is like that: we use fragments such as “rights”, “utility” and “duty” torn from the traditions that once gave them sense, so moral debates end in assertion against assertion. Virtues are the qualities needed to achieve the goods internal to practices such as chess, farming or medicine, to give a life the unity of a story, and to sustain a living tradition. The choice, he concluded, is “Nietzsche or Aristotle?”, and he chose Aristotle, and later Aquinas.
