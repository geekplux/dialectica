Continental · 1905–1980

Jean-Paul Sartre

Parisian novelist and philosopher, the public face of existentialism

Sartre argued that consciousness is a nothingness that can always negate what is given, so humans are radically free and responsible; later he tried to reconcile this freedom with Marxism.

Life and thought

Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980) studied at the École normale supérieure in Paris, where he met Simone de Beauvoir, his lifelong partner. He discovered phenomenology in Berlin in 1933–34, made his name with the novel Nausea (1938) and was a prisoner of war in 1940–41. After the Liberation he became perhaps the most famous intellectual in the world and co-founded the journal Les Temps modernes; in 1964 he declined the Nobel Prize in Literature.

Being and Nothingness starts from consciousness, which is not a thing but a power to negate: we can always imagine what is not, so we are never simply what we are. Hence “existence precedes essence”: there is no fixed human nature or God-given purpose, and we define ourselves by our choices. This freedom is a burden, and we flee it in “bad faith”, like a waiter playing at being a waiter as if he had no choice. In the later Critique of Dialectical Reason he tried to fit this freedom into Marxist history.

On the record

Contested

Beauvoir influenced Sartre’s philosophy.

How far is debated. Beauvoir herself called her philosophy an elaboration of Sartre’s, but since the 1990s scholars have asked whether ideas in Being and Nothingness (1943) first appeared in her novel She Came to Stay (1943); her diaries show ideas developed before, alongside and often against his.
