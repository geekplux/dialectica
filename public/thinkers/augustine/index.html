Medieval Europe · 354–430

Augustine of Hippo

North African bishop who fused Christianity with Platonism

Evil is a privation of good, freely chosen by a will turned away from God; we find truth by turning inward, where God illuminates the mind.

Life and thought

Augustine was born in 354 in Thagaste, in Roman North Africa, to a pagan father and a Christian mother, Monica. He taught rhetoric in Carthage, Rome and Milan, spent nine years among the Manichaeans, and passed through scepticism before reading Neoplatonist books and hearing Bishop Ambrose. After his conversion in 386 he returned to Africa, became bishop of Hippo in about 395 and died in 430 as the Vandals besieged the city.

His Confessions tell this search from the inside. Two ideas from it shaped the West. Evil is not a substance competing with God, as the Manichaeans held, but a lack of good: a will that turns from the highest good to lesser ones. And truth is found by turning inward: certain truths, like those of number, cannot come from the changing senses, so God must illuminate the mind. The City of God, written after the sack of Rome in 410, read all history as two loves at war.

On the record

Contested

Descartes drew on Augustine’s “if I am mistaken, I exist”; how far is debated.

Arnauld’s Fourth Objections (1641) cited Augustine’s On Free Choice 2.3 as a source of the cogito, and in 1640 Colvius pointed Descartes to City of God 11.26; Descartes thanked them but acknowledged no debt and stressed his different use of the argument. Recent scholarship, notably Stephen Menn’s Descartes and Augustine (1998), argues the debt was extensive. The tie is kept as influence, with the dispute stated.
