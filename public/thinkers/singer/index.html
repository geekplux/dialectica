Peter Singer

Australian utilitarian of animal liberation and effective giving

Equal interests deserve equal consideration whoever has them, so the suffering of animals counts, and if we can prevent something very bad without sacrificing anything of comparable importance, we ought to do it.

Life and thought

Peter Singer (born 1946 in Melbourne) is the son of Viennese Jews who fled to Australia in 1938; his grandparents were among the victims of the Holocaust. He studied in Melbourne and at Oxford under R. M. Hare, founded the Centre for Human Bioethics at Monash University, and from 1999 taught at Princeton, where he is now emeritus. He co-founded The Life You Can Save and helped inspire the effective-altruism movement.

Singer is a utilitarian: what matters morally is the interests of every being that can suffer or enjoy, counted equally. In “Famine, Affluence, and Morality” (1972) he argued that if you would wade into a pond to save a drowning child at the cost of ruining your clothes, you ought equally to give money that would save a child far away, because distance makes no moral difference. Animal Liberation (1975) extended equal consideration to animals and, using Richard Ryder’s term, condemned “speciesism”, helping to launch the modern animal-rights movement. His views on euthanasia and on severely disabled newborns have provoked fierce protests.
