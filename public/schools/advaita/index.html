India · c. 6th c. CE – today

Advaita Vedānta

Brahman, pure consciousness, is the only reality; the self is Brahman, and the world of many things is an appearance produced by ignorance (avidyā).

The question

If the self is Brahman, why do we experience a world of many things?

Why it arose

Vedānta (‘the end of the Veda’) schools interpreted the Upanishads, the Bhagavad Gītā and the Brahma Sūtra. Gauḍapāda and Śaṅkara read them as teaching strict non-duality, in sharp debate with Buddhists and with Mīmāṃsakas, whose ritual-first view Śaṅkara rejected.

The reasoning

Only what never changes and is never negated is ultimately real; objects come and go, but the awareness to which they appear is never absent. As a rope in dim light is mistaken for a snake, Brahman is mistaken for the world and the self for a limited person; knowledge removes the error, as seeing the rope removes the snake.

Key claims
  1. Brahman alone is real; the world is appearance.
  2. The individual self is not different from Brahman.
  3. Liberation comes from knowledge, not ritual action.
What it left

It became the best-known Indian philosophy, shaping Hindu monastic orders and, through Vivekananda and others, modern global ideas of Hindu spirituality.
