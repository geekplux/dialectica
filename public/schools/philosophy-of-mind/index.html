Analytic philosophy of mind

The mind is studied as part of nature: mental states are explained by what brains do, while consciousness remains the hardest test of that picture.

The question

How can a physical brain have thoughts and feelings?

Why it arose

Behaviourism and Ryle’s attack on the “ghost in the machine” left Cartesian dualism unfashionable, while computers and neuroscience suggested the mind might be explained physically. From the 1950s, philosophers asked what kind of physical explanation could actually work.

The reasoning

Functionalism (Putnam) holds that a mental state is defined by its causal role, not its material, so the same mind could in principle run on neurons or silicon. Critics answer that something is left out: Searle’s Chinese Room says symbol manipulation is not understanding, and Nagel and Chalmers argue that the felt quality of experience, “what it is like”, resists functional explanation. Dennett replies that this “hard problem” is an illusion produced by our intuitions.

Key claims
  1. Mental states are defined by their functional roles.
  2. Manipulating symbols is not the same as understanding them.
  3. Explaining subjective experience is the “hard problem”.
What it left

It laid the conceptual foundations of cognitive science and frames today’s arguments about whether AI systems can think or feel.
