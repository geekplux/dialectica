China & East Asia · 3rd–5th c. CE

Xuanxue (“Profound Learning”)

Behind the many things lies a formless root, or, as Guo Xiang replied, things simply arise of themselves; the sage combines Confucian conduct with Daoist insight.

The question

What is the root of all things, and how does the sage live?

Why it arose

After the Han dynasty fell in 220, state Confucianism, with its omens and heavy commentaries, lost credibility amid political danger. Scholars turned to the “Three Profound Texts”, the Laozi, Zhuangzi and Book of Changes, and to “pure conversation” (qingtan) in search of a deeper metaphysics.

The reasoning

Wang Bi argued that anything with a definite form cannot be the source of all forms, so the root must be formless “non-being” (wu). Guo Xiang replied that non-being cannot produce anything, so each thing “self-generates” (zisheng) and is fulfilled by following its own nature.

Key claims
  1. Non-being is the root of being (Wang Bi).
  2. Things arise spontaneously of themselves (Guo Xiang).
  3. Once the meaning is grasped, words and images can be forgotten.
What it left

It gave Chinese thought an abstract vocabulary, such as substance and function (ti/yong) and being and non-being, used to interpret Buddhism and later taken up by the Neo-Confucians.
