Neo-Confucianism (Cheng–Zhu school)

All things share one principle (li) expressed through material force (qi); by investigating things and cultivating the mind, a person recovers original goodness and can become a sage.

The question

How can Confucian ethics rest on a full account of cosmos and mind?

Why it arose

For centuries Buddhism and Daoism had offered sophisticated metaphysics and meditation, while Confucianism looked like mere social ethics. Song scholars such as Zhou Dunyi, Zhang Zai and the Cheng brothers built a Confucian metaphysics of their own; Zhu Xi synthesised it, and from 1313 his commentaries on the Four Books were the standard for the civil-service examinations.

The reasoning

Everything has a principle that makes it what it is, and all principles are ultimately one. Human nature is that principle, so it is originally good; evil comes from turbid qi and selfish desires. By “investigating things” (gewu) and holding reverent seriousness (jing), one clarifies one’s qi and realises one’s nature.

Key claims
  1. Principle (li) and material force (qi) together make all things.
  2. Human nature is principle, and therefore good.
  3. Investigate things to extend knowledge.
What it left

It was the orthodoxy of late imperial China, Joseon Korea and, in part, Tokugawa Japan for some six centuries; Korean scholars refined it in debates such as the Four–Seven debate.
