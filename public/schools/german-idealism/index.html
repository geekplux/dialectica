Early modern Europe · 1790s – 1840s

German Idealism

Reality is not a heap of dead things but the self-unfolding activity of reason or spirit, which comes to know itself through nature and history.

The question

If the mind shapes experience, can it also know the absolute?

Why it arose

Kant left two gaps: unknowable “things in themselves” behind appearances, and a split between nature ruled by necessity and humans as free moral agents. Young philosophers of the 1790s, stirred by the French Revolution and a revival of interest in Spinoza, set out to close these gaps with a single system grounded in freedom.

The reasoning

If the thing in itself is unknowable, we have no right even to say it exists; so the forms of thought are not merely ours but the forms of reality. Fichte began from the self’s free activity, Schelling from an absolute in which nature and mind are one, and Hegel argued that reality is a rational process that develops through contradictions, each position revealing its limits and being overcome, until spirit understands itself.

Key claims
  1. What is rational is actual, and what is actual is rational.
  2. Truth unfolds historically through contradiction and its resolution.
  3. Freedom is realised in social institutions, not in isolated individuals.
What it left

Hegel’s dialectic passed, turned upside down, to Marx, and provoked Kierkegaard and Schopenhauer; British Idealism ruled Oxford until Russell and Moore revolted against it.
