Liberal theories of justice (Rawls and after)

A just society is one whose basic institutions free and equal people could agree to: protecting basic liberties, and allowing inequalities only when they benefit the worst-off.

The question

What principles should govern a fair society?

Why it arose

By the 1960s utilitarianism dominated Anglo-American moral and political thought, while the civil-rights movement and arguments over the welfare state made justice an urgent question. Rawls revived the social-contract tradition to show what utilitarianism missed: that each person’s life is separate and cannot simply be traded off for a larger total.

The reasoning

Imagine choosing the rules of society behind a “veil of ignorance”, not knowing your class, talents, race or idea of the good life. Rational people would secure equal basic liberties and accept inequalities only if they help the least advantaged (the “difference principle”). Nozick objected that any such pattern violates people’s rights to what they freely acquire; Nussbaum, with Amartya Sen, asked instead what people are actually able to do and to be.

Key claims
  1. Justice is the first virtue of social institutions.
  2. Fair principles are those chosen behind a veil of ignorance.
  3. Rights and capabilities matter, not just total welfare.
What it left

It revived political philosophy as a field and supplied the vocabulary of today’s debates on welfare, rights and global justice; the capabilities approach influenced the UN’s Human Development Index.
