Kyoto School

Reality is grasped not as an object but through “pure experience” and “absolute nothingness”, a field in which self and world arise together.

The question

Can Western philosophy be rethought from the Buddhist insight into nothingness?

Why it arose

Meiji Japan absorbed Western philosophy at speed. At Kyoto Imperial University, Nishida Kitarō, a long-time Zen practitioner, set out to state Zen-inflected insights in the language of James, Bergson, Hegel and the Neo-Kantians; colleagues and students such as Tanabe Hajime and Nishitani Keiji developed and contested his ideas.

The reasoning

Before the split into subject and object there is immediate “pure experience”. Whatever is determined as a being stands in some “place” (basho); the ultimate place cannot itself be a being, so it must be “absolute nothingness”, in which self and world are both located and define each other.

Key claims
  1. Pure experience comes before subject and object.
  2. Absolute nothingness is the ultimate “place” of all beings.
  3. Nihilism is overcome by passing through it to emptiness.
What it left

The first major modern non-Western school in sustained dialogue with European philosophy, it remains central to comparative philosophy and Buddhist–Christian dialogue, though some members’ wartime nationalist writings are still debated.

On the record

Corrected

The Kyoto School is drawn from 1911 to 1990, the year Nishitani died.

The school was never a formal organisation; it is usually described as philosophers spanning several generations: Nishida and Tanabe; then Hisamatsu, Nishitani and others; then Takeuchi, Tsujimura and Ueda. The Stanford Encyclopedia describes it today as both an object of scholarship and a living tradition, so it is now drawn to the present.

Sources: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “The Kyoto School”
