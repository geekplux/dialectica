Early modern Europe · 1789 – today

Utilitarianism

The right action is the one that produces the greatest total happiness, with everyone’s happiness counting equally.

The question

What makes an action or a law right?

Why it arose

Late eighteenth-century Britain had a chaotic legal system full of savage punishments defended by custom and tradition. Bentham, a legal reformer who had read Hume, Helvétius and Beccaria, wanted a single rational test by which every law and institution could be judged.

The reasoning

Pleasure and pain are what we all, in the end, care about, so the sensible measure of a law or act is its effect on happiness. Since no one’s happiness matters more than anyone else’s, we should add up the consequences for all affected and choose the greatest net good. Mill refined this, holding that some pleasures are higher than others and that liberty itself serves the general good.

Key claims
  1. Only consequences matter in judging actions and laws.
  2. Each to count for one, and none for more than one.
  3. Any being that can suffer deserves moral consideration.
What it left

It drove reforms of law, prisons, voting and welfare, underlies cost–benefit reasoning in economics and policy, and through Peter Singer inspired the animal-rights and effective-altruism movements.

On the record

Confirmed

Bentham wrote that reading Hume made him feel “as if scales had fallen from my eyes”.

In a footnote to A Fragment on Government (1776) Bentham says that the third volume of Hume’s Treatise demonstrates that the foundations of all virtue lie in utility, and that on reading it he “felt as if scales had fallen from my eyes”.
