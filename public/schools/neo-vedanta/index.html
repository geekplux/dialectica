Neo-Vedānta & modern Indian thought

The Vedāntic insight that all beings share one divine reality should be lived out as practical service, religious tolerance and national renewal.

The question

What can India’s ancient philosophy offer a colonised, modernising world?

Why it arose

Under British rule, Indian intellectuals faced missionary criticism and Western science. Reformers from Ram Mohan Roy onward, then Vivekananda and Aurobindo, recast Vedānta as a rational, universal spirituality that could also ground social reform and nationalism.

The reasoning

If every person is ultimately divine, then serving people is serving God, caste and sectarian divisions lose their basis, and the religions are many paths to one truth. Aurobindo added an evolutionary turn: consciousness hidden in matter is unfolding through life and mind toward a higher ‘supermind’, so spiritual life should transform the world rather than flee it.

Key claims
  1. All religions are paths to the same truth.
  2. Service to humanity is worship of the divine.
  3. Spirit evolves through matter toward higher consciousness (Aurobindo).
What it left

It shaped Indian nationalism and the worldwide image of yoga and Vedānta; critics argue it reshaped classical Advaita in Western, Protestant-influenced terms.
