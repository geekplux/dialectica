China & East Asia · c. 380–250 BCE

School of Names

The relation between names and reality is puzzling, and careful argument and paradox can show how language carves up the world.

The question

How do names relate to the things they name?

Why it arose

In an age of fierce debate between schools, a group of “disputers” (bianzhe) specialised in argument itself. Hui Shi, a statesman of Wei and Zhuangzi’s friend, posed paradoxes about space and time, and Gongsun Long argued that “a white horse is not a horse”.

The reasoning

A name that picks out a combination, such as “white horse”, is not identical with a name that picks out a broader kind, such as “horse”. Close analysis of terms shows that our judgements of sameness and difference depend on which aspect, and which scale, we attend to.

Key claims
  1. “A white horse is not a horse.”
  2. Sameness and difference are relative to the level of comparison.
  3. Love all things; heaven and earth are one body.
What it left

Dismissed for centuries as hair-splitting sophistry and quickly marginalised, it is now studied as early Chinese logic and philosophy of language.

On the record

Corrected

The “All Under Heaven” chapter of the Zhuangzi, which preserves Hui Shi’s paradoxes, is listed as Zhuangzi’s work of c. 250 BCE.

Since Guo Xiang, only the Inner Chapters have been associated with Zhuangzi himself; “All Under Heaven” is chapter 33, in the Miscellaneous group, and probably dates from the first decades of the Han dynasty. Hui Shi’s own writings are lost.

Sources: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “School of Names” · Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Zhuangzi”

Contested

Mohism is linked to the School of Names as a borrowing: the Mohist canons answer paradoxes like “a white horse is not a horse”.

The later Mohist Dialectics contain passages that seem to rebut the disputers’ ideas, and the Zhuangzi’s “All Under Heaven” shows Mohist sects debating the same “hard and white” and “same and different” themes. Who influenced whom is not recorded; the line marks shared problems.

Sources: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “School of Names”
