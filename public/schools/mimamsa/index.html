India · c. 2nd c. BCE – 18th c. CE

Mīmāṃsā

The Veda is eternal and authorless, its injunctions are the only source of knowledge about duty (dharma), and any cognition is valid unless proved otherwise.

The question

How do we know our duty, and why trust the Veda?

Why it arose

As Vedic ritual grew complex and was challenged by Buddhists and Jains, ritual specialists needed rules for interpreting the texts and a defence of their authority. Jaimini’s sūtras set out those rules; in the 7th century Kumārila Bhaṭṭa and Prabhākara turned them into a full theory of knowledge.

The reasoning

Duty concerns what ought to be done and what results it brings later, which no perception can reach, so it can be known only from a text. A text with no author has no author’s errors or deceptions, and since no author of the Veda is remembered, it is authorless and reliable. And every cognition must be valid by itself (svataḥ prāmāṇya) unless defeated—otherwise we would need an endless chain of checks.

Key claims
  1. The Veda is eternal and authorless (apauruṣeya).
  2. Dharma is known only from Vedic injunction.
  3. Cognitions are intrinsically valid until defeated.
What it left

Its rules of interpretation underlie Hindu legal reasoning (dharmaśāstra) and Vedāntic exegesis, and its default trust in cognition anticipates modern debates on testimony and justification.
