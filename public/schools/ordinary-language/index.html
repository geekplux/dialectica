Analytic & pragmatist · c. 1945 – 1970

Ordinary language philosophy

Philosophical puzzles arise when words are taken out of their everyday use; looking closely at how language is actually used dissolves them.

The question

What do our words mean in the way we actually use them?

Why it arose

Wittgenstein, back in Cambridge from 1929, came to reject the picture of language in his own Tractatus. After 1945, Oxford philosophers such as Gilbert Ryle and J. L. Austin, equally wary of ideal logical languages, turned to the fine grain of everyday speech.

The reasoning

The meaning of a word is its use within a practice (a “language-game”), not an object it stands for. Philosophers create problems, such as whether the mind is a ghostly thing inside the body, by stretching words beyond the contexts that give them sense. Map the actual uses, and the problem often disappears.

Key claims
  1. The meaning of a word is its use in a form of life.
  2. Many philosophical problems are confusions of language.
  3. Speaking is doing: utterances are actions.
What it left

It gave us speech-act theory, developed by Austin and then Searle, shaped linguistic pragmatics, and left philosophy with a lasting habit of attending carefully to usage.
