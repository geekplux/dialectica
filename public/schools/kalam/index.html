The Islamic world · 8th c. – present

Kalām (Muʿtazila & Ashʿarism)

Faith must be defended by rational argument; the Muʿtazila made God’s justice and unity the measure, while the Ashʿarites made God’s unlimited power and will the measure.

The question

Is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good?

Why it arose

Early Islamic disputes after the civil wars, over free will, the status of grave sinners and God’s attributes, met the arguments of Christian, Jewish and Manichaean debaters. Muslims developed a dialectical theology to answer them; the Abbasid inquisition (833–848) briefly made Muʿtazili doctrine state policy.

The reasoning

The Muʿtazila argued that a just God cannot punish acts humans do not freely choose, and that reason can know good and evil. Al-Ashʿari replied that nothing limits God: God creates every event at each instant, so causes are only God’s habits, and good is whatever God commands.

Key claims
  1. Muʿtazila: reason can know good and evil without revelation.
  2. Ashʿarites: God directly creates every event; causes are only habits.
  3. The world was created in time, out of nothing.
What it left

Ashʿarism, alongside Māturīdism, became the main theology of Sunni Islam, and Muʿtazili ideas lived on in Shiʿi and Jewish thought; its occasionalism anticipated later European debates on causation.

On the record

Confirmed

Kalām is dated from the 8th century, with Wāṣil ibn ʿAṭāʾ (d. 748).

This is a convention. Sunni sources trace the Muʿtazila to Wāṣil’s withdrawal from the circle of al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī in Basra over the status of the grave sinner, but the story of the name is told rather than documented, and the Encyclopaedia of Islam explains the name differently. Arguments about free will and the sinner (the Qadariyya and Murjiʾa) already ran in the late 7th century, so the start date marks when kalām became a recognisable school, not its first debates.

Sources: Wikipedia, “Muʿtazilism” (citing the Encyclopaedia of Islam)

Contested

Kalām atomism echoes Greek atomism.

Maimonides already traced kalām atomism to the Greeks; 19th-century scholars proposed Indian sources instead. Shlomo Pines found real resemblances but judged the question of origin unresolved, and Alnoor Dhanani compared kalām atoms to Epicurean minimal parts without identifying a route of transmission. Kalām built its arguments from paradoxes of the infinite, in its own way; the link is drawn as a resemblance whose path is unknown, which the note states.

Sources: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Arabic and Islamic Natural Philosophy and Natural Science” · Iqbal Review (1991), on the sources of kalām atomism
