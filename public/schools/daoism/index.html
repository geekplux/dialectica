China & East Asia · c. 4th c. BCE – today

Daoism

The Dao, the nameless way of all things, works without forcing; people flourish by returning to spontaneity and acting through non-action (wuwei).

The question

How can we live in accord with the way things naturally go?

Why it arose

While the Warring States schools competed to offer rules for restoring order, Daoist texts questioned the whole project of names, rules and striving, which they saw as a cause of disorder. The Laozi (Daodejing) took shape over the 4th and 3rd centuries BCE, and the writings gathered under Zhuangzi’s name developed a playful, sceptical voice.

The reasoning

Every distinction we make, such as good and bad or useful and useless, depends on its opposite and on a point of view. Imposing such distinctions on the world distorts its natural course. So the sage acts without forcing, and the best ruler interferes least.

Key claims
  1. The Dao that can be spoken of is not the constant Dao.
  2. Softness and yielding overcome hardness.
  3. All distinctions depend on a point of view.
What it left

It shaped Chinese religion, painting, poetry, medicine and political thought, and fed Xuanxue and Chan Buddhism; the Daodejing is among the most translated books in the world.

Where it came from

Reacted against
ConfucianismThe Daodejing treats Confucian virtues as symptoms of decline: “when the great Dao is abandoned, there are humaneness and righteousness.”
