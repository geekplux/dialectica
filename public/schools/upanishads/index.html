India · c. 800–200 BCE

Vedic & Upanishadic thought

The innermost self (ātman) is one with the ultimate reality (Brahman), and knowing this frees a person from the cycle of rebirth.

The question

What is the one reality behind everything, and what am I?

Why it arose

Late Vedic ritual specialists began asking what gave the sacrifice its power, and some teachers turned inward, linking the order of the cosmos to breath, mind and self. In the growing towns of the Ganges plain, where wandering renouncers questioned ritual, death, rebirth (saṃsāra) and release moved to the centre of thought.

The reasoning

Everything we see changes, so something must underlie the change; and the seer cannot itself be seen, so the self that knows is not any object it knows. If that knowing self is the very ground of the world, then grasping it ends the fear and bondage that come from mistaking oneself for a body.

Key claims
  1. Brahman is the single reality behind the many.
  2. The self is not body or mind but their inner witness.
  3. Knowledge, not ritual alone, liberates from rebirth.
What it left

The Upanishads became the scriptural foundation of Vedānta and set the shared vocabulary—self, karma, rebirth, liberation—that Buddhists and Jains also argued over.

What it led to

Opposed by
Cārvāka / Lokāyata materialismCārvākas rejected the Upanishadic self that survives death, and with it the whole scheme of karma and rebirth.Opposed by
Jain philosophyJains shared the new ideas of karma and liberation but rejected Vedic authority and any single world-soul, positing countless individual souls instead.Opposed by
Early Buddhism & AbhidharmaThe Buddha kept karma and rebirth but denied the permanent ātman that the Upanishads identified with Brahman.Carried on by
Sāṃkhya & YogaSāṃkhya took up the Upanishadic search for the true self but split reality into many conscious selves and one material nature instead of a single Brahman.Carried on by
Advaita VedāntaŚaṅkara read the Upanishadic identity of ātman and Brahman in the strictest sense: there is only one reality.Carried on by
Theistic Vedānta (Viśiṣṭādvaita & Dvaita)Rāmānuja and Madhva reread the same Upanishads as speaking of a personal God distinct from, or qualified by, souls and the world.
