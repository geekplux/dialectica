Continental · 1966 – today

Post-structuralism

Structures of meaning and knowledge are never closed or neutral: they shift, exclude and exercise power, so no foundation escapes critique.

The question

Can any system of meaning or knowledge be stable, neutral or final?

Why it arose

By the mid-1960s structuralism dominated Paris, and its promise of a science of culture met the upheavals of decolonisation and May 1968. Thinkers trained in it, and in Nietzsche, Heidegger and Freud, began asking what structuralism itself assumed: a fixed centre, a neutral observer, an order outside history.

The reasoning

If meaning arises from differences, it is never fully present in any sign but always deferred to other signs (Derrida’s différance), so texts undo their own oppositions. Foucault argued that what counts as knowledge, madness or normality is produced by historical practices bound up with power. The task, then, is not to find the true foundation but to show how apparent necessities were made and could be otherwise.

Key claims
  1. Meaning is always deferred and never fully present.
  2. Knowledge and power produce each other.
  3. Grand narratives of progress deserve incredulity.
What it left

It reshaped literary studies, gender and queer theory, postcolonial studies and the history of science, and remains a lightning rod in debates about truth and relativism.
