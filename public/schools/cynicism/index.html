Greece & Rome · c. 400 BCE – 5th c. CE

Cynicism

Virtue alone is enough for happiness, and it is found by living simply according to nature, scorning wealth, status and social convention.

The question

What do we really need to live well?

Why it arose

Antisthenes, a follower of Socrates, took his master’s indifference to comfort and opinion as the heart of philosophy. Diogenes of Sinope, exiled and poor, turned it into public performance in Athens and Corinth, living in a storage jar and mocking the powerful; the name comes from kyōn, “dog”.

The reasoning

If virtue is all that matters for happiness, as Socrates suggested, then everything else, such as money, fame, family and city, is at best indifferent and often a chain. Animals live well with almost nothing; so can we, once we strip away artificial needs. Shamelessness becomes a teaching tool that exposes how arbitrary conventions are.

Key claims
  1. Virtue is sufficient for happiness.
  2. Live according to nature, not convention.
  3. “I am a citizen of the world” (kosmopolitēs).
What it left

Cynicism handed Stoicism its ethical core and coined the ideal of the cosmopolitan; its provocative style lives on in satire and protest, though modern “cynical” means something quite different.

Where it came from

Grew out of
Socratic philosophyThe Cynics took Socrates’ poverty, frankness and indifference to opinion as the whole of philosophy, dropping his theoretical inquiries.

What it led to

Carried on by
StoicismZeno studied with the Cynic Crates and kept the Cynic view that virtue alone suffices for happiness, while restoring logic and physics.

On the record

Confirmed

No primary texts by Antisthenes or Diogenes survive, so the Cynic works list is empty.

Ancient catalogues list many Cynic writings, but none survives; Cynicism is known through anecdotes and sayings, above all in Diogenes Laertius, Book 6.
