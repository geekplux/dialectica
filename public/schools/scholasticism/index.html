Dialectica / Schools / Scholasticism

Medieval Europe · c. 1070 – early 17th c.

Scholasticism

Reason, trained by Aristotle’s logic, can prove much of what faith believes and clarify the rest, because truth cannot contradict truth.

The question

How far can reason go in understanding what faith holds?

Why it arose

Cathedral schools and then the new universities of Paris and Oxford needed a method for teaching theology. From the 12th century, Latin translations of Aristotle and of Arabic and Jewish philosophers arrived, and scholars had to fit this powerful pagan system into Christian doctrine.

The reasoning

God is the author of both nature and scripture, so what reason demonstrates and what revelation teaches must agree. The way to resolve apparent conflicts is the disputation: state the objections, give the authorities, then distinguish senses until the contradiction disappears.

Key claims
  1. Faith and reason are distinct but cannot truly conflict.
  2. God’s existence can be shown by argument.
  3. Hard questions are settled by disputation and careful distinctions.
What it left

It created the university disputation and much of Western philosophical vocabulary; Thomism was revived in the 19th century and is still taught.

Where it came from

Grew out of
Patristic & Augustinian philosophyAnselm took Augustine’s method of “faith seeking understanding” and turned it into rigorous argument.Borrowed from
AristotelianismThe recovery of Aristotle’s full corpus in the 12th–13th centuries gave the schools their logic, physics and metaphysics.Borrowed from
Falsafa (Arabic Aristotelianism)Latin translations made in Toledo and elsewhere brought Avicenna’s essence–existence distinction and Averroes’ commentaries into the universities.Borrowed from
Medieval Jewish philosophyAquinas cites “Rabbi Moses” (Maimonides) on negative theology and on why the world’s eternity cannot be disproved.

On the record

Corrected

Scholasticism ran from c. 1070 to the 16th century, ending with Suárez.

Suárez died in 1617, and the Stanford Encyclopedia stresses that his death “does not mark the end of this university tradition”: Iberian “baroque” scholasticism continued, and some historians would end medieval Latin philosophy only around 1700. The atlas now ends the block in the early 17th century, after Suárez, and treats later school philosophy as its afterlife.

Sources: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Medieval Philosophy”
