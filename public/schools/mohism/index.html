Dialectica / Schools / Mohism

China & East Asia · c. 430–200 BCE

Mohism

Care for everyone impartially, and judge every doctrine and practice by whether it benefits the people.

The question

What standard tells us which practices are right?

Why it arose

Mozi, probably of humble artisan background, lived through war and hardship and rejected the Confucians’ graded love and costly rites such as elaborate funerals and music. His followers formed a disciplined organisation that also served as defensive military engineers for besieged states.

The reasoning

Disorder arises because people care for their own family and state more than others’. If everyone cared impartially, theft and aggression would lose their point. Any doctrine should pass three tests: the precedent of the sage kings, the evidence of ordinary people’s eyes and ears, and its benefit to the people.

Key claims
  1. Practise impartial care for all (jian ai).
  2. Wars of aggression are unjust.
  3. Wasteful rites and music harm the people.
What it left

It faded after the Qin–Han unification, but its later canons contain China’s earliest systematic logic, optics and mechanics, and it was rediscovered by reformers in the 19th and 20th centuries.

On the record

Corrected

The Mozi’s “Impartial Care” and “Against Offensive War” chapters are listed as Mozi’s work.

The Mozi is a collection of anonymous texts from the 5th to the 3rd century BCE. The core chapters, each surviving in up to three versions, record the doctrines of Mozi’s movement as written down by his followers, not by Mozi himself.
