Post-positivist philosophy of science

Science does not grow by piling up verified facts: theories are tested as wholes, can be refuted but never finally proven, and change through historical upheavals.

The question

How does science actually gain knowledge?

Why it arose

The logical positivists hoped to ground science on verification, but no finite set of observations can verify a universal law, and the line between “observational” and “theoretical” kept blurring. Between the 1930s and the 1960s, Popper, Quine and Kuhn dismantled that picture from three different directions.

The reasoning

Popper: no number of white swans proves “all swans are white”, but one black swan refutes it, so science advances by bold conjectures and attempted refutations. Quine: a test never confronts a single sentence, only a whole web of belief, so even logic is in principle revisable. Kuhn: “normal science” works inside a paradigm until anomalies pile up and a revolution replaces it.

Key claims
  1. Falsifiability, not verifiability, marks a scientific theory.
  2. Beliefs face experience as a whole web, not one by one.
  3. Science changes through paradigm shifts, not only accumulation.
What it left

It made the history and sociology of science central to philosophy of science, and put “falsifiable” and “paradigm shift” into everyday language.

PragmatismQuine ended “Two Dogmas” by calling for a “more thorough pragmatism”: we revise our web of belief by what works best.
