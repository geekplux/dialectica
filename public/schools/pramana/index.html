Buddhist epistemology (Pramāṇa school)

There are only two valid means of knowledge: perception, which grasps unique particulars, and inference, which works through general concepts.

The question

What counts as valid knowledge, and how does sound reasoning work?

Why it arose

Public debates between Buddhists, Nyāya logicians and Mīmāṃsakas required shared rules of proof. Dignāga, working from Yogācāra and earlier Nyāya logic, reorganised the field around valid cognition (pramāṇa); Dharmakīrti deepened it about a century later.

The reasoning

Perception is free of concepts and reaches only momentary particulars; concepts work by exclusion (apoha)—‘cow’ means ‘not non-cow’—so universals are mental constructions, not real things. A valid inference needs a reason present in the subject, present in similar cases and absent from dissimilar ones, and Dharmakīrti grounds that necessity in real relations of identity and causation.

Key claims
  1. Only two means of knowledge: perception and inference.
  2. Universals are conceptual exclusions, not real entities.
  3. Valid cognition is what reliably guides successful action.
What it left

It set the agenda for Indian epistemology for centuries—Nyāya and Mīmāṃsā wrote against it—and remains central to Tibetan monastic education; in China its logic became known as yinming.
