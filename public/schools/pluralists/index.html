Greece & Rome · c. 460–428 BCE

Pluralists

Parmenides was right that nothing comes from nothing, but the world is many unchanging elements whose mixing and separating is what we call change.

The question

How can change be real if nothing comes from nothing?

Why it arose

After Parmenides, simply naming one changing stuff was no longer an option. Empedocles in Sicily and Anaxagoras, who brought philosophy to Athens, accepted the Eleatic ban on absolute coming-to-be but tried to save the visible world by multiplying the ultimate realities.

The reasoning

If each basic ingredient is eternal and unchanging, nothing truly comes into being or perishes; only arrangements change. Empedocles named four “roots” (earth, water, air and fire) moved by Love and Strife. Anaxagoras held that “in everything there is a portion of everything”, set in motion by Mind (nous).

Key claims
  1. There are many eternal elements, not one.
  2. Change is the mixing and separating of what already exists.
  3. A moving cause is needed: Love and Strife, or Mind.
What it left

Empedocles’ four elements dominated European and Islamic science until the 17th century, and Anaxagoras’ cosmic Mind prompted Socrates and Plato to look for purpose in nature.

What it led to

Carried on by
AtomismAtomism pushed the pluralists’ strategy, explaining change as the rearrangement of permanent units, to its limit: countless atoms differing only in shape, order and position.
