Medieval Europe · c. 2nd – 8th c. CE

Patristic & Augustinian philosophy

Christian faith can be understood with the tools of Greek philosophy, above all Platonism: God is the eternal Good, and evil is a lack of good.

The question

How can revealed faith and pagan reason be reconciled?

Why it arose

As Christianity spread through an educated Roman world, its teachers had to defend it against pagan critics and settle disputes about God, Christ and evil. The philosophical language at hand was Greek, especially the Platonism of Plotinus and Porphyry.

The reasoning

If God is wholly good and created everything, evil cannot be a substance in its own right; it must be a privation, a good thing turned away from its source by a free will. Truth, unchanging and shared by all minds, points beyond the mind to God, who illuminates it.

Key claims
  1. Evil is the privation of good, not a thing in itself.
  2. Faith comes first, and understanding follows from it.
  3. God sees all of time at once, in an eternal present.
What it left

Augustine shaped Latin theology, the medieval idea of the self and later debates on grace and will; Boethius handed Aristotle’s logic to the Latin Middle Ages.

Where it came from

On the record

Confirmed

Patristic philosophy is drawn as ending around 750, with John of Damascus.

This is a convention, and a reasonable one: John of Damascus (c. 660–c. 750) is usually counted the last of the Greek Church Fathers. Historians draw the line between late antiquity and medieval philosophy in several places (c. 500, c. 800, or with Augustine himself), so the boundary should be read as a hinge rather than an ending.
