Greece & Rome · c. 300 BCE – 3rd c. CE

Stoicism

Virtue is the only good; by living in agreement with nature’s rational order and judging events rightly, we can be free and at peace whatever happens.

The question

How can we be happy in a world we cannot control?

Why it arose

Zeno of Citium, a merchant who by ancient anecdote was shipwrecked near Athens, studied with the Cynic Crates and with Platonists before teaching in the Painted Porch (Stoa Poikilē) around 300 BCE. In the uncertain Hellenistic world, and later in Rome, Stoicism offered a complete system of logic, physics and ethics for living under fortune.

The reasoning

The cosmos is ordered by logos, a divine reason, and we share in that reason. Health, wealth and reputation can be taken away, so they are not truly good; only our own judgements and choices are fully up to us. Passions such as fear and anger are mistaken judgements, so by correcting our judgements we can reach apatheia, freedom from disturbing passion.

Key claims
  1. Virtue is the only good; externals are “indifferent”.
  2. Distinguish what is up to us from what is not.
  3. All humans share reason and form one world community.
What it left

It shaped Roman law, Christian ethics and the ideas of natural law and universal human dignity; today it is revived in cognitive behavioural therapy and popular “Stoic” self-help.

Where it came from

Grew out of
CynicismZeno studied with the Cynic Crates and kept the Cynic view that virtue alone suffices for happiness, while restoring logic and physics.Grew out of
Socratic philosophyThe Stoics saw themselves as Socratics: virtue is knowledge, and the sage is the person who never assents to a false judgement.

What it led to

Opposed by

On the record

Corrected

Zeno of Citium was a merchant shipwrecked near Athens.

The shipwreck is an ancient anecdote with several versions: Diogenes Laertius reports that Zeno was wrecked carrying purple dye to Piraeus, but also that some say he heard of the wreck while already in Athens, or sold his cargo before turning to philosophy. The atlas now presents it as a story.

Sources: Diogenes Laertius, Lives of the Eminent Philosophers 7.2–5 (trans. R. D. Hicks, LacusCurtius)
