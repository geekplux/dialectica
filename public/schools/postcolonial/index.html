Postcolonial thought

Colonialism ruled minds as well as lands, so liberation requires undoing the categories through which the West described and governed others.

The question

How did empire shape the way we know and see others?

Why it arose

After 1945, struggles for independence swept Asia and Africa. Fanon, a psychiatrist from Martinique working in Algeria during its war against France, analysed racism and colonial violence from the inside; later Said and Spivak, teaching in American universities, turned literary and philosophical theory on the archive of empire.

The reasoning

Colonial rule depended on knowledge: maps, grammars, censuses and novels that described the colonised as backward, timeless or silent. If that knowledge was a form of power, independence alone would not end it; it survives in self-images, school curricula and Western scholarship, and must be dismantled by critique.

Key claims
  1. Colonialism dehumanises both colonised and coloniser.
  2. “The Orient” is a Western construction serving power.
  3. The subaltern is spoken for, rarely heard.
What it left

It transformed literary studies, history and anthropology, and feeds today’s debates on decolonising universities, museums and philosophy itself.
