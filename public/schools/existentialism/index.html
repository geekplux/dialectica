Existentialism

Existence precedes essence: humans are not born with a fixed nature but make themselves through free choices for which they alone are responsible.

The question

How should I live if no given nature or order tells me?

Why it arose

Kierkegaard rebelled against Hegel’s all-embracing system, which he thought left no room for the anxious, choosing individual; Nietzsche announced that “God is dead”. After two world wars, occupation and the Holocaust, French writers such as Sartre, Beauvoir and Camus turned these themes into a philosophy of freedom in a world without given meaning.

The reasoning

A paper knife is designed before it is made, so its essence comes first. If there is no designer of human nature, Sartre argued, we exist first and define ourselves afterwards by what we do. So we cannot blame nature, God or society for our choices; pretending we have no choice is “bad faith”.

Key claims
  1. Existence precedes essence.
  2. We are condemned to be free and responsible for what we make of ourselves.
  3. Anxiety and absurdity are honest responses to a world without given meaning.
What it left

It shaped postwar literature, theatre, psychotherapy and feminism, and its vocabulary of authenticity, anxiety and the absurd entered everyday speech.

On the record

Confirmed

Existentialism is drawn from 1843 to 1980: forerunners in the 1840s, a heyday in the 1940s–1960s.

These bounds are a convention, as the label is open-ended: the word was introduced by Gabriel Marcel in 1943, Kierkegaard and Nietzsche are its acknowledged forerunners, and it peaked in post-war France. 1843 is Kierkegaard’s Either/Or and 1980 the death of Sartre; the movement still has academic heirs.
