Phenomenology

Philosophy should begin by describing experience exactly as it is lived, setting aside assumptions about what lies behind it.

The question

How do things show up for us in experience?

Why it arose

Around 1900, psychology was claiming logic and knowledge as its own territory, and positivists held that only natural science gives truth. Husserl, trained in mathematics and taught by Franz Brentano, wanted a rigorous science of consciousness itself, under the rallying cry “back to the things themselves”.

The reasoning

All consciousness is consciousness of something (intentionality), so mind and world are always given together. If we suspend (“bracket”) the question of whether things exist independently, we can describe the structures through which anything appears at all: perception, time, the body, other people. Those structures come before, and underlie, the theories of science.

Key claims
  1. Consciousness is always directed at something.
  2. Suspend assumptions and describe what appears.
  3. Lived experience comes before scientific abstraction.
What it left

Through Heidegger, Sartre, Merleau-Ponty and Levinas it became the backbone of continental philosophy, and it still informs cognitive science, nursing and studies of embodiment.
