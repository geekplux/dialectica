China & East Asia · 12th – late 19th c.

School of Mind (Lu–Wang school)

Moral principle is not out in things but in one’s own mind; everyone has innate good knowing (liangzhi), and true knowing is inseparable from acting.

The question

Where is moral principle found: in things, or in the mind?

Why it arose

Lu Jiuyuan debated Zhu Xi at Goose Lake Temple in 1175, objecting that Zhu’s step-by-step study of external things fragmented moral life. Three centuries later Wang Yangming, after failing to find principle by contemplating bamboo and then enduring exile in Guizhou, had an awakening in 1508 that the mind itself is principle.

The reasoning

Filial piety is not found in one’s parent but in one’s own caring mind, so moral principles belong to the mind itself. Everyone already has innate good knowing; and to know the good without doing it is not yet to know it, so knowing and acting form one unity.

Key claims
  1. The mind is principle (xin ji li).
  2. Knowing and acting are one.
  3. Extend your innate good knowing.
What it left

It swept through late Ming China and, as Yōmeigaku, inspired activists in Japan up to the Meiji Restoration; twentieth-century New Confucians revived it.

On the record

Corrected

The School of Mind is drawn from the 12th to the 17th century, ending in 1700.

Wang Yangming learning (Yōmeigaku) went on in Japan: Nakae Tōju (1608–1648) and Kumazawa Banzan (1619–1691) in the 17th century, and a line of “Yangmingists” from Ōshio Heihachirō’s Osaka uprising of 1837 into the Meiji era and beyond. The end date is moved to the late 19th century.

Sources: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Japanese Confucian Philosophy” · University of Tokyo, review of Kojima Tsuyoshi, The Wang Yangming School in Modern Japan
