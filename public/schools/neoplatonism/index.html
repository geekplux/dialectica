Greece & Rome · c. 245 – 6th c. CE

Neoplatonism

All reality flows from a single, ineffable source, the One, through Intellect and Soul down to matter, and the soul’s task is to turn back and return to it.

The question

How does the many come from the One, and how do we return?

Why it arose

In 3rd-century Rome, amid political crisis and competing salvation religions, Plotinus reread Plato as a unified system, absorbing Aristotle’s theory of intellect. His pupil Porphyry edited his treatises, and later teachers in Athens and Alexandria, such as Proclus and Hypatia, made it the last great school of pagan philosophy.

The reasoning

Every multiplicity depends on a unity that holds it together, so the first principle must be absolutely one, beyond being and thought. It does not create by choice but overflows, as light radiates from the sun, producing Intellect (the realm of Forms), which produces Soul, which produces the visible world. Because we came from the One, we can return through philosophy, virtue and contemplation.

Key claims
  1. The One, beyond being, is the source of all things.
  2. Reality is a hierarchy of emanation: One, Intellect, Soul, matter.
  3. The soul can ascend back to union with the One.
What it left

Through Augustine, Pseudo-Dionysius, al-Farabi and Avicenna it became the metaphysical framework of medieval Christian, Islamic and Jewish thought, and it inspired Renaissance Florence and many mystical traditions.

Where it came from

Grew out of
PlatonismPlotinus read Plato’s dialogues as one system, placing the Good of the Republic, now called the One, at the summit of reality.Borrowed from
AristotelianismPlotinus built Aristotle’s self-thinking divine intellect into his second level, Intellect, and Porphyry made Aristotle’s logic the standard introduction to Platonism.Grew out of
PythagoreanismLater Neoplatonists such as Iamblichus and Proclus revered Pythagoras and gave number a central place in the unfolding of reality.
