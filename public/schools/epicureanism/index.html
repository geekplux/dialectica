Greece & Rome · 306 BCE – 3rd c. CE

Epicureanism

The goal of life is pleasure understood as tranquillity: freedom from bodily pain and mental fear, reached through simple living, friendship and a true understanding of nature.

The question

How can we live free from fear and pain?

Why it arose

After Alexander’s death the Greek world was ruled by vast, unstable kingdoms, and citizens had little control over public life. Around 306 BCE Epicurus founded a community, the Garden, outside Athens, open to women and slaves, offering a therapy for anxiety built on Democritus’ atomism.

The reasoning

Everything, including the soul, is atoms that disperse at death, so “death is nothing to us”: where death is, we are not. The gods, if they exist, do not meddle in the world, so there is nothing to fear from them either. Remove these fears and limit desires to the natural and necessary, and what remains is ataraxia, a stable and deep pleasure.

Key claims
  1. Pleasure, as absence of pain and fear, is the good.
  2. Death is nothing to us.
  3. Friendship is among the greatest goods.
What it left

Lucretius’ poem preserved it for the Renaissance, where its rediscovery fed early modern science and secular ethics; utilitarianism inherits its view that pleasure and pain are what matter.

Where it came from

Grew out of
AtomismEpicurus adopted Democritus’ atoms and void; later sources, chiefly Lucretius and Cicero, add that he gave atoms a slight “swerve” to make room for free choice.

What it led to

Carried on by
Social contract theoryEpicurus had called justice a pact neither to harm nor be harmed; the contract theorists likewise explained law as an agreement serving each person’s interest.Carried on by
UtilitarianismLike Epicurus, utilitarians take pleasure and freedom from pain as the only things good in themselves, but they count everyone’s happiness, not just one’s own.

Its answers to the big questions

EthicsHow should one live?Seek quiet pleasure: freedom from pain and fear, simple needs and good friends.

On the record

Corrected

Epicurus added a slight “swerve” of atoms to make room for free choice.

The swerve is not described in Epicurus’ surviving letters; it is known chiefly from Lucretius and Cicero, who attribute it to him. Scholars generally accept it as Epicurean doctrine, but its exact form is unclear.
