Dialectica / Schools / Socratic philosophy

Greece & Rome · c. 430–399 BCE

Socratic philosophy

The unexamined life is not worth living: by questioning what we think we know about virtue, we discover our ignorance and begin to live well.

The question

How should one live, and what is virtue?

Why it arose

Socrates turned philosophy from the heavens to human life amid Athens’ war with Sparta, political upheaval and the sophists’ claim to teach virtue for money. He wrote nothing; we know him through Plato, Xenophon and Aristophanes. In 399 BCE Athens executed him for impiety and corrupting the young.

The reasoning

People act badly because they mistake what is good for them; no one does wrong knowingly. So virtue is a kind of knowledge, and the way to it is the elenchus: ask someone to define courage or justice, show that their answers contradict each other, and the shared perplexity clears the ground for real understanding.

Key claims
  1. Virtue is knowledge; no one does wrong willingly.
  2. Wisdom begins in recognising one’s own ignorance.
  3. Care for the soul matters more than wealth or reputation.
What it left

Almost every later Greek school claimed Socrates as its ancestor, and his questioning method remains the model of philosophical dialogue and teaching.
